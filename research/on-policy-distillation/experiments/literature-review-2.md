Self-Distilled Reasoner: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Language Models – Siyan Zhao et al. (2026, arXiv:2601.18734)[1]. This paper introduces OPSD (On-Policy Self-Distillation), where a single LLM plays both teacher and student by conditioning the teacher on privileged information (e.g. the correct answer or reasoning trace) and the student on the problem only[1]. It relates to our work as a baseline on-policy distillation method, but differs in that the teacher here uses explicit ground-truth answers rather than generating any analysis of the student’s output. Threat: LOW (standard on-policy KD with answer-only PI, no deliberative reasoning).
Self-Distillation Enables Continual Learning – Idan Shenfeld et al. (2026, arXiv:2601.19897)[2]. SDFT uses a demonstration-conditioned model as teacher and the same model (without demos) as student, generating on-policy training signals from in-context examples[2]. It is similar in spirit to our use of context to guide distillation, but the teacher here simply conditions on fixed demonstration examples rather than analyzing the student’s own rollout. Thus, it lacks any teacher-generated reasoning about student errors. Threat: LOW (leverages in-context demos for distillation, not per-example teacher analysis).
On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes (GKD) – Rishabh Agarwal et al. (2024, arXiv:2306.13649)[3]. This work (Generalized KD) also addresses on-policy distillation by training the student on its own sampled outputs with dense teacher feedback, mitigating train–inference mismatch[3]. It is a direct predecessor in on-policy KD, but it does not incorporate any chain-of-thought or internal reasoning from the teacher – the teacher only provides token-probability feedback. Threat: LOW (standard on-policy KD framework, no teacher introspection).
Speculative Knowledge Distillation (SKD) – Wenda Xu et al. (2024, arXiv:2410.11325)[4]. SKD uses interleaved sampling: the student proposes tokens and the teacher “replaces poorly ranked ones” based on its distribution[4]. This is akin to a “best-of-N” approach where low-quality student tokens are filtered. It partly resembles our best-of-N deliberation, but the teacher still does not generate a reasoning trace – it only assesses tokens. Threat: MEDIUM (uses teacher filtering on student outputs, somewhat related, but no explicit reasoning/coaching).
Privileged Information Distillation for Language Models (π-Distill) – Emiliano Peñaloza et al. (2026, arXiv:2602.04942)[5]. This work trains a teacher–student pair where the teacher is conditioned on privileged information (e.g. environment actions) and the student is not[5]. It also explores an on-policy self-distillation variant. It is related through the use of privileged context in training, but here the teacher never “reasons about the student’s solution” – it is given PI from a stronger model or environment, not the student’s output. Threat: MEDIUM (uses PI-conditioned teacher, but teacher analysis is external rather than introspective).
Reinforcement-Aware Knowledge Distillation (RLAD) – Zhaoyang Zhang et al. (2026, arXiv:2602.22495)[6]. RLAD focuses on distillation during RL by doing selective imitation (trust-region KL) only when it benefits the policy[6]. It introduces a PPO-like ratio objective instead of a raw KL. While it deals with on-policy distillation under RL, it still uses standard teacher outputs and KL losses – no pre-scoring reasoning or multi-lens. Threat: LOW (distillation in RL context, unrelated to teacher-generated analysis).
Process Reward Models That Think (ThinkPRM) – Muhammad Khalifa et al. (2025, arXiv:2504.16828)[7]. ThinkPRM is about step-by-step verification models that produce a chain-of-thought to verify each solution step[7]. It is relevant in that it uses CoT for step-level evaluation, but its output is a scalar verification score (for RL reward), not a token-wise distribution signal. Threat: LOW (chain-of-thought verifier, not a teacher scoring student with distributions).
Alternating Reinforcement Learning for Rubric-Based Reward Modeling (Rubric-ARM) – Ran Xu et al. (2026, arXiv:2602.01511)[8]. Rubric-ARM jointly trains a rubric generator and a judge via alternating RL to produce multi-criteria rewards[8]. This uses multi-dimensional structured feedback (rubrics) to evaluate outputs. It parallels our interest in multi-criteria credit assignment, but it produces scalar or preference labels, not token-level teaching signals. Threat: LOW (rubric-based reward modeling, not a distributional distillation method).
“The Whole Is Greater Than the Sum of Its Parts”: COMPACT – Jin Cui et al. (2026, arXiv:2601.13992)[9]. COMPACT proposes multi-teacher CoT distillation: dynamically fusing diverse teacher rationales via a compatibility-aware weighting of teacher gradients[9]. It relates in that it exploits multiple reasoning “views”, but it still relies on merging fixed teacher CoTs (different teachers), not on a teacher generating a new analysis for a given student output. Threat: MEDIUM (multi-lens distillation is related conceptually, but no introspective teacher analysis of student answers).
Mind’s Mirror: Distilling Self-Evaluation Capability and Comprehensive Thinking – Weize Liu et al. (2024, arXiv:2311.09214)[10]. Mind’s Mirror trains students on teacher-generated self-evaluation outputs and multiple chain-of-thoughts, teaching students to judge and reason about solutions[10]. This is closer to our “teacher analyzes reasoning” idea, but the teacher is reflecting on its own reasoning rather than on a student’s answer. The privileged signal is still teacher’s self-critique of its CoT. Threat: MEDIUM (uses generated self-evaluations to distill critical thinking, but doesn’t target student-generated answers).
Symbolic Chain-of-Thought Distillation (SCoTD) – Liunian H. Li et al. (2024, arXiv:2306.14050)[11]. SCoTD trains a small model on many chain-of-thoughts sampled from a large teacher[11]. It shows small models can “think” by distilling teacher rationales. While it is a form of CoT distillation, it does not involve any teacher reasoning about the student’s own output; the teacher simply provides its CoTs on problems. Threat: LOW (explicit CoT distillation baseline, not teacher critique of student answers).
Each of the above is cited to show how it connects or differs from our “deliberative teaching” approach[1][5]. None exactly duplicates our idea of having the teacher first generate an analysis of the student’s actual solution before scoring it, so the threat to novelty is generally LOW or at most MEDIUM (for related concepts like multi-criteria or self-evaluation).
